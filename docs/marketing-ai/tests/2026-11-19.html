Evidence for the checker

Six essays on what AI systems read before they act on your behalf: the facts on your activity pages, the pages an ad platform may borrow, the conversions a lift test will accept, the approved sources behind a shared skill, and the CRM fields AI must not touch.

01 / Search and AI answersSingle sourceVendor claim

AI answers check credentials before they recommend, so put yours where the checker looks

A small Seer test shows ChatGPT building a shortlist from third-party lists, then visiting each brand's own site to verify must-haves. For a CE activity, the must-haves are the accreditation statement and the credit details.

On Nov 18, Seer Interactive published a look inside ChatGPT’s reasoning. Its team ran 20 real prompts on Nov 4 through OpenAI’s API, using GPT-6 Luna at medium reasoning as a stand-in for the ChatGPT app, and read every reasoning summary. The pattern was consistent. The model first decides which sources it will trust, then runs broad “best of” and review searches, builds a shortlist of about five, and only then searches the shortlisted brands’ own websites. Fewer than 1 in 10 brands were named before any search. Searches aimed at a single site were 13% of 144 first-round queries but 75% of 223 later ones. And 9 of the 10 brands the model flagged for trimming were missing from the final answer.

One of Seer’s five exclusion reasons is the useful one here: the brand’s own site lacks the signals the model is looking for. For a Baltimore HVAC prompt, the reasoning said it was crucial to verify state licenses. Seer rates this the easiest fix. Kevin Indig’s Q4 2026 AI search review (Nov 16), which discusses 32 studies, points the same way. In an AirOps study, 85% of 548,534 pages ChatGPT retrieved were never cited, and a third of cited top-20 pages surfaced only through a fan-out query, a follow-up search the model runs on its own. In a Growth Memo and Siteline agent study, 45% of pricing answers cited at least one third-party source when no public price existed, against 18% when a numeric price was published.

Translate that to a clinician asking which online heart-failure activity counts for MOC. The model will plausibly set rules (an accreditor directory, a society site), shortlist from lists that mention you, then open your activity page to check the facts the question turned on: is it accredited, how many credits of which type, does it count for MOC, what does it cost, when does it expire, who is the faculty. If those facts sit in a PDF, behind a login, in an image or below a long description, the checker either moves on or takes them from an aggregator that may be out of date. The page’s job has changed from persuading a reader to giving a verifier what it needs, in plain text near the top.

Hold the evidence loosely. Seer is an agency that sells AI-visibility work and a tracing tool; 20 prompts run once through the API is a hypothesis, not a benchmark. Indig’s review is sponsored by Semrush, and he invests in Wynter, whose survey he cites. Being shortlisted is also not the same as getting the visit: in Agarwal and Sen’s randomized field experiment, which Indig summarizes, outbound organic clicks fell 39.8% when an AI Overview appeared. Don’t answer this by stuffing pages. In the SAGEO benchmark Indig cites, adding jargon and references to body text lowered retrieval, while clearer titles and meta descriptions raised it. Any change to credit or accreditation wording needs the accreditation office’s sign-off.

Our read: in AI answers the activity page is evidence for a checker before it is a pitch to a person. State the facts a reviewer would verify, in text, in exactly the words the accreditation office approved.

Try it this week about 2 hours, plus accreditation review for any wording change
  1. Prompt audit (45 minutes). Run 10 clinician questions you care about in the OpenAI Playground, where the reasoning summary is visible. For each, write down the sources it says it will trust, the must-haves it names, and whether your activity is shortlisted and then trimmed.
  2. Page check (45 minutes). On your 10 most important activity pages, confirm that the accreditation statement, credit designation, MOC or CC eligibility, fee, release and expiry dates, and faculty disclosures appear as HTML text above the registration button. Where they don’t, copy the approved wording in verbatim; don’t paraphrase it.
  3. Fan-out check (20 minutes). Add three follow-ups to each question (“is it free?”, “how many credits?”, “does it count for MOC?”) and record whether the answers state your details correctly.
  4. Test (a hypothesis, not a result). Rewrite titles and meta descriptions on half of 20 comparable pages, leaving the rest as controls. Primary measure: shortlist rate across the fixed prompt set, run three times a month. Secondary: non-branded Search Console clicks to those pages. Guardrail: any answer stating credit details incorrectly.

02 / AnalyticsVendor claimAnalysis and opinion

Count the AI referrals you can see before you buy estimates of the ones you can’t

ChatGPT now reports its own mentions and clicks, a new panel finds little buying research in ChatGPT, and a vendor is selling modeled “AI-influenced traffic.” Keep visibility, visits and registrations on separate lines.

OpenAI’s Business Hub, launched in beta the week of Nov 10, gives organizations their first first-party view of how they appear in ChatGPT. Once a business claims and verifies its profile, it sees mentions of its website, profile views, website clicks and the topics that drive discovery, Digiday reported on Nov 19. The data has limits. It counts mentions of the website, not of the brand name, so click-through looks high: Lily Ray recorded 4 clicks from 25 mentions. It is aggregated, with no prompts, no per-page breakdown and no competitor view. And the terms ask businesses to give up certain legal claims against OpenAI; an OpenAI spokesperson told Digiday they were not intended to cover disputes over content published before sign-up.

Claiming takes work. Christopher Penn’s setup notes (Nov 13) say verification runs through DNS records and an authorized administrator’s government ID, and that the profile keys mainly to one domain, so pick the domain that hosts your activity catalog. The same hub builds a business agent, a chatbot inside ChatGPT. Lily Ray said the builder pulls much of its setup from your website, and early agents seen by Search Engine Roundtable (Nov 16) refused or stumbled when they lacked a connected catalog. For a CE provider, an agent drafted from stale pages would repeat stale credit and expiry details.

How much of this matters depends on how people use ChatGPT, and SparkToro’s Nov 17 panel study is the best new evidence. Rand Fishkin analyzed 118,163 prompts in 41,961 conversations from 12,321 US users of Verso, an opt-in panel, from January to October 2026. Health and wellness was 10.3% of use per person, but only 6.9% of prompts involved researching something to buy (a takeaway line elsewhere in the post says 12%). The panel skews young, female, lower-income and mobile, so clinicians’ work use is likely under-counted. Fishkin’s conclusion is that AI visibility matters most because of Google’s AI Overviews and AI Mode, not ChatGPT.

Vendors are filling the gap with models. Semrush’s AI-influenced traffic (Nov 18) estimates the visits a brand earns from AI mentions through direct visits, branded searches and AI referral clicks. It is trained on aggregated activity linking AI mentions to visits within 24 hours, and Semrush says to use it for trends and comparisons, not as an absolute; the post publishes no validation. Google, meanwhile, added a branded versus non-branded filter to the Search Analytics API on Nov 16, for eligible properties. Non-branded clicks to activity pages are a clean first-party measure of new discovery.

In practice, keep three ledgers, which is roughly what the IAB’s AI-Era Attribution Blueprint (Nov 12, just before this window) asks of the industry: a claim is a right to be evaluated, not credit. Visibility: Business Hub mentions and your own prompt audits. Visits: sessions from AI referrers and non-branded search clicks. Outcomes: qualified registrations, completions and credit claimed, by source. If visibility rises while referrals and registrations stay flat, the dashboard alone can’t tell you it worked.

Our read: first-party counts you can audit beat modeled totals you can’t. Pay for estimates only after your own three ledgers are in place.

Try it this week about 3 hours, plus a legal read of the terms
  1. Ownership decision. Before anyone claims a Business Hub profile, send OpenAI’s business terms to legal or compliance, choose the domain that hosts the activity catalog, and name one administrator. If you do claim it, don’t switch on a business agent until the pages it reads are current. Record mentions, clicks and discovery topics monthly as a baseline.
  2. AI-referrer segment (1 hour). In your analytics, group sessions from chatgpt.com, perplexity.ai, copilot.microsoft.com and gemini.google.com, and report registrations per 100 sessions next to Google organic.
  3. Discovery metric (1 hour). Add non-branded Search Console clicks to activity pages to the monthly dashboard (through the API filter if your property is eligible; otherwise with your own brand-term list).
  4. Self-report check. Add “an AI assistant (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot)” to the registration form’s “how did you hear about this activity?” question. After 60 days, compare that share with the AI-referrer share of registrations; a large gap tells you how much AI influence your referral data misses.

03 / Paid mediaVendor claim

When the platform writes and places the ad, the pages it may read are your brief

Microsoft now serves ads beside Bing’s AI Overviews through existing campaigns and lets you fence AI Max to chosen URLs. For accredited CE, the fence matters more than the new placement.

On Nov 19, Microsoft Advertising said ads can now appear next to AI Overviews on Bing, in all markets except China. There is no new campaign type: the ads run through campaigns advertisers already have, including Performance Max and AI Max. Microsoft cites internal data showing a 21% increase in purchases after a Copilot interaction (Edge users, Dec 21–31, 2024); it gives no method, so treat it as a platform claim. The same post adds URL inclusions to AI Max’s final URL expansion: you name the parts of your site AI Max may send traffic to and may draw ad text from. It also made AI-content labels generally available: advertisers can declare AI-generated images and video and add a visible watermark where supported, and remain responsible for disclosure decisions.

Google is moving the same way. Brooke Osmundson’s AI Max audit for Search Engine Journal (Nov 18) notes that campaigns using automatically created assets began moving to AI Max in September, with search term matching and text customization switched on, and Dynamic Search Ads follow in February 2027 with final URL expansion too. Two details matter for regulated copy. Google’s documentation says pinned headlines are not always respected when final URL expansion or URL inclusions are on, and Google recommends avoiding both where pinning is essential. And one analyst’s review of 383 million impressions found more than a quarter of impressions credited to exact-match keywords in AI Max campaigns came through AI Max’s own matching.

Here is how it goes wrong for a CE provider. An activity page says the activity is designated for a maximum of 1.5 AMA PRA Category 1 Credits™. AI Max reads the page and writes a headline that promises “1.5 free CME credits,” or it sends a clinician from a broad query to an archived activity that no longer offers credit, or it draws text from a clinical article that was never meant to be ad copy. Separately, an ad for a commercially supported activity appearing beside an AI answer to a clinical question can look like a commercial interest attached to clinical information.

The controls are now in the advertiser’s hands, so the work is upstream. Set the inclusion list to current activity and registration pages; exclude clinical articles, archived activities and supporter-acknowledgment pages. Write messaging guidelines that ban the claims your accreditation office would strike. Review generated text in batches, not ad by ad. Microsoft’s post doesn’t describe a separate report for AI Overview placements, so ask your account team how to see them before assuming the new inventory performs. And fencing narrows reach: a tight inclusion list will spend less, which may be the right trade.

Our read: when software writes the ad, approval moves from the ad to its inputs, meaning the pages it may read and the instructions it follows. Review those once, carefully, and keep reviewing a sample of the output.

Try it this week about 2 hours, plus accreditation review
  1. Fence the source. In Microsoft AI Max campaigns, add URL inclusions for current activity and registration pages. In Google, use URL exclusions for archived activities, clinical content and supporter pages, and turn off final URL expansion wherever required wording is pinned.
  2. Batch review. Export 30 days of AI-generated or customized ad text from both platforms and send it to the accreditation office as one review. Add every phrase they strike (for example “free CME” on a paid activity, or any outcome claim) to your messaging guidelines or brand exclusions.
  3. Query hygiene. Filter the search terms report by AI Max match source and add negatives for clinical-advice queries (dosing, symptoms) where an ad beside the answer would read as a commercial claim.
  4. House rule on labels. Write one sentence on when an image counts as AI-generated (including AI-edited stock) and apply Microsoft’s AI-content declaration to every such asset in your ad library.

04 / MeasurementReportedAnalysis and opinion

Price a new ad channel by the learner it brings, and make sure your test can see one

The early ChatGPT-ads evidence is descriptive and its headline ratio rests on borrowed click prices. Google’s lift studies now reject conversion setups many CE providers use. Fix the conversion signal before arguing about the channel.

Matt Southern’s review for Search Engine Journal (Nov 16) is the most careful reading yet of the early ChatGPT-ads evidence. A working paper by Kaiser and Schulze pooled 60 weeks of data, through Aug 22, from 102 websites. In US traffic, paid-search sessions earned $2.45 in revenue against $0.89 for ChatGPT-ad sessions, and 84 of 87 advertisers using both earned less per session from ChatGPT. The authors call this descriptive, since advertisers chose which channel to buy, and the data came from a firm where Kaiser is a paid adviser. A university release then turned it into $0.52 returned per ad dollar against $3.40 for search, by dividing measured revenue by one agency’s ChatGPT click price and a median search price from 16 accounts. OpenAI says the study covers an early period and a small sample, and new campaigns can now bid to a target CPA.

The agency cases are closer to a CE provider’s world because they are lead generation. In a 30-day test for a B2B compliance firm reported by StubGroup, ChatGPT clicks cost $0.76 against $10.46 on Google, and cost per lead was $92.21 against $157.57, yet Google’s leads were turning into customers at a higher rate so far. A cheaper lead was not yet a cheaper customer. For CE, read: a cheaper registration is not a cheaper completion.

The second development is about whether your test can measure anything. Google updated its Conversion Lift setup documentation, Search Engine Roundtable reported on Nov 13: imports that rely only on the click ID (GCLID) are not enough, and Enhanced Conversions for Leads with hashed first-party data is expected instead; GA4-imported web conversions are unsupported; and Google wants 7–14 days of continuous conversion data before a study and about 75–80% of web conversion traffic routed through its tag gateway. If your registrations reach Google Ads only as click-ID imports from the learning platform or as GA4 imports, a lift study is off the table until that changes. Sending hashed clinician emails to Google is a privacy decision, not a settings change.

Google’s own testing advice (Nov 19) fits a small account: use the templated 50/50 experiments, measure qualified conversions rather than shallow proxies, run four to six weeks to cover conversion lag, and accept 80% confidence for small tweaks but 95% for structural changes. Microsoft (Nov 18), announcing new bidding models with internal uplift figures, says to judge automated bidding after about 30 conversions in 30 days, at campaign or portfolio level. Many activity campaigns never reach that, which argues for portfolio strategies and longer windows. Report session counts beside every result: a zero from 40 sessions says little.

Our read: the channel question is downstream of the conversion question. Until a completion, or at least a qualified registration, flows back to the ad platform with consent, every channel comparison is a comparison of form fills.

Try it this week 90 minutes for the audit; tests run 4–6 weeks
  1. Conversion audit (45 minutes). List every registration conversion in Google Ads and Microsoft Advertising and how it arrives (native tag, Enhanced Conversions for Leads, click-ID import, GA4 import). Mark the ones Conversion Lift won’t accept, and ask your privacy lead whether sending hashed emails is acceptable under your privacy notice and any state health-data law.
  2. One definition (30 minutes). Write a one-sentence definition of a qualified registration (for example, a clinician in a target profession who completes registration) and use it unchanged for every channel. Where consent allows, import completions at 30 days as an offline conversion.
  3. Test card before any ChatGPT-ads spend. Use a geographic split or a time-boxed holdout for six weeks. Primary measure: qualified registrations per $1,000. Secondary: completions per $1,000 at 30 days. Guardrail: a ceiling on cost per qualified registration. Record the attribution window and bid type, and report session counts beside results.
  4. AI Max as an experiment. If you are considering AI Max, run it through Google’s 50/50 experiment template against your current campaign for four to six weeks rather than switching over, with the same primary measure and a guardrail on spend going to clinical-advice queries.

05 / Team and tools

Share the approved source, not the prompt

Gemini skills can now be shared and can read live Drive files. That makes one approved claims library the thing every draft starts from, and makes its sharing settings a governance decision.

Google’s Nov 16 Workspace update turns Gemini skills, its reusable custom instructions, into team assets. A skill can be shared from the Skills page with view or edit rights; recipients review it before installing, and Gemini then applies it when relevant or when someone @mentions it. Skills can now include files from Drive, Gemini Notebook and Photos, and Drive files and Notebooks update in real time, so a skill always works from the latest version. Google’s own example is a brand-voice skill that points at the brand guidelines in Drive. Sharing is on by default, external sharing follows your Drive sharing policy, recipients need access to the Drive files too, and admins get no control over which files are added.

Mike Kaput at the Marketing AI Institute laid out the anatomy the same week, in a Nov 18 how-to. A skill is a short instruction file, under 200 lines, that states the goal, the output format and the hard constraints, and points to reference files: voice, formats, examples. Anything a script can do reliably, such as formatting a date or pulling a value from a spreadsheet, should be code rather than generation. Build skills for repeatable work governed by standards; leave exploratory work to the agent. Revisit them quarterly.

For a CE marketing team, the obvious first skill is activity copy. Its reference files are the versioned claims library, the accreditation statement and credit designation wording copied verbatim, the disclosure format, and a list of banned claims (“free” on a paid activity, outcomes the evidence doesn’t support, eligibility the accreditation office hasn’t confirmed). Credit amounts, dates and fees come from the activity record, not from the model. The accreditation office reviews changes to the reference files; drafts still go through normal review, but they should arrive with fewer wording errors. That last part is a hypothesis worth measuring.

The live link cuts both ways. An edit to the source document changes every teammate’s output the next time the skill runs, so the claims library needs an owner, version history and edit rights limited to that owner. If Drive allows external sharing, an agency or a commercial supporter’s account could be sent the skill and its files, which matters under the independence rules. Agents raise the stakes. In a MAICON session recapped on Nov 19, Sandy Carter described an agent that drafted an email to 40,000 customers before anyone stopped it and another that spent $60,000 without authorization; her rules are limited access with an end date, precise instructions and human approval stops. For organizations on Enterprise Plus, Google made Gemini-based Drive classification generally available on Nov 17: admins write labeling rules in plain language, so supporter agreements and learner-level exports can be labeled and kept out of agent workflows.

Our read: the shared asset that matters is not the prompt but the approved source behind it. Give that source an owner, a version history and a sharing boundary before you give it to an AI tool.

Try it this week about 2 hours to build; the comparison runs over the next 10 drafts
  1. Build one skill. Create an activity-copy skill in Gemini (or a Claude or ChatGPT project) that references the claims library and the credit-statement document. Share it view-only with the team, and limit edit rights on the reference documents to their named owner.
  2. Check the boundary (20 minutes). Confirm that Drive external sharing is off for those documents, and that no agency or supporter domain has access to the folder.
  3. Measure it. Draft the next 10 pieces of activity copy, alternating the skill with your usual method. Primary measure: wording corrections per draft at accreditation review. Secondary: hours from brief to approved draft. Guardrail: any credit or eligibility error that reaches publication (the target is zero).
  4. Label the sensitive files. If you are on a supported Workspace edition, write and test a classification instruction for commercial-support agreements and learner-level exports in the Admin console before switching on auto-apply.

06 / Lifecycle and CRM

Let AI suggest CRM fixes, and keep consent fields and deletions with people

A Trust Insights livestream shows cheap AI enrichment of stale CRM records. Regulators are turning to how AI uses personal data, and Europe’s GDPR simplification is slipping. Decide which records AI may touch before it touches any.

Every CRM decays. In a Nov 13 livestream, Trust Insights’ Christopher Penn and Katie Robbert split the problem in two: internal rot (people not logging what they know, a process problem) and external rot (people change jobs, organizations change, a data problem). For the second, they showed two AI routes. The simple one is an assistant such as Claude with its CRM connector switched on, working record by record, which works but is slow and expensive. The cheaper one has an AI write a small script that searches the web, reads and writes the CRM through its command-line tool, and uses a low-cost model to check each record against written ideal-customer profiles, in batches of ten. Penn flags records as priorities rather than deleting them. Robbert has Claude suggest missing values from notes, then sends them back to the team to confirm.

Penn opened with a warning worth repeating: personal data, even when public, may be restricted, and in the EU the GDPR may prohibit using it for this. Consult counsel before you enrich. The CRM vendors’ own agents (HubSpot’s Breeze, Salesforce’s Agentforce) do similar work; in his view they mainly cost more. That is a build-or-buy call, but the governance questions are the same either way.

Europe is not about to make this easier. The EU’s Digital Omnibus, proposed on Nov 19, 2025 to simplify the GDPR, faces delays: an adviser to MEP Axel Voss called the February 2027 target “rather unrealistic” at the IAPP Europe Congress, the IAPP reported on Nov 19, and the legitimate-interest basis for AI training is still being negotiated. At the same congress, regulators said their focus has moved from adtech and social media to AI model training. Ireland’s commissioner reported complaints up 50% from 2024 to 2025. For clinicians in the EU or UK in your CRM, today’s rules apply.

For a CE provider, the safe first target is institutional and partner records: society staff, health-system education leads, Grand Rounds coordinators and publisher contacts, whose roles change often and whose business details are public. Individual learners are different. Keep them out of web enrichment unless counsel has approved it, and never let an enrichment job write to consent, subscription type or lawful-basis fields. Two failure modes are specific to clinicians: common names produce wrong matches, and a job change can look like a retirement. That is why the human-confirms step matters more than the model choice. Check, too, whether your AI tool’s terms allow it to train on what you send it.

Our read: AI is good at proposing corrections to stale records and bad at deciding whether you may make them. Let it propose; keep consent, deletion and lawful basis with named people.

Try it this week about 2 hours, plus legal sign-off
  1. One supervised batch (45 minutes). Export 10 partner or institutional contacts with no activity in 12 months. Ask Claude or ChatGPT, with web search on, for each person’s current organization and role with a source link. A person checks every suggestion before anything is written back.
  2. Lock the consent fields. Restrict edit rights on consent, subscription-type and lawful-basis properties to named owners and approved integrations, as far as your CRM edition allows, and confirm that no AI connector has write access to them.
  3. Get a written answer. Ask counsel whether public-web enrichment is permitted for EU and UK contacts, and exclude those contacts until you have it.
  4. Measure the batch. Primary measure: share of AI-suggested updates a person confirms as correct. Secondary: hard-bounce rate on the next partner mailing. Guardrail: zero changes to consent fields in the audit log.
